# Violence Against Women Act (VAWA)

## Overview

The Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) is a U.S. federal law first enacted in 1994 to improve responses to domestic violence, sexual assault, dating violence, and stalking. It supports coordinated efforts across federal, state, tribal, and local systems.

VAWA programs are administered primarily through the U.S. Departments of Justice (DOJ) and Health and Human Services (HHS), and provide funding, guidance, and infrastructure to support victim services and community responses.

---

## What VAWA Supports

VAWA focuses on strengthening system-level responses rather than directly measuring incidents. Key areas of support include:

* Funding for victim services and advocacy programs
* Legal assistance for survivors
* Housing support and protections
* Prevention and education initiatives
* Coordination between law enforcement, courts, and service providers

These efforts aim to improve access to safety, justice, and support for survivors.

---

## 2022 Reauthorization Highlights

VAWA is reauthorized periodically to expand and update its provisions. The 2022 reauthorization introduced several important updates, including:

* Expanded access to housing assistance and emergency support
* Increased funding for culturally specific service providers
* Support for non-carceral and community-based response options
* Restoration of certain tribal jurisdiction authorities
* Additional resources for prevention and survivor services

These updates reflect ongoing efforts to adapt the law to current needs and improve access to services.

---

## Interpretation Notes

* VAWA describes **policy and support systems**, not incident-level data.
* It complements—but is separate from—data sources like NIBRS used in this system.
* Changes in VAWA policy do not directly translate to changes in reported incident counts.

---

## Summary

VAWA is a foundational federal law that supports coordinated responses to violence through funding, services, and legal protections. It provides important context for understanding how systems respond to violence, but it should not be interpreted as a measure of the prevalence of incidents.
